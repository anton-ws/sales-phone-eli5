The phone game, explained like you're 5

What we learned about our sales calls, and what we did about it. 21 to 29 September 2026.

WebScribble sales team. Numbers as of 29 Sep 2026, 2:00 PM ET. We only looked. We did not change anything in Apollo, Trellus or Salesforce.

Our salespeople make lots of calls every day. But almost every call reaches a robot, not a person.

Imagine this

You want to talk to your friend Sam.

Sam has a phone of his own. But the grown-up who gives you phone numbers keeps giving you the number of Sam's whole school.

You call the school. A robot says: "Press 1 for the office. Press 2 for the library." Or a busy secretary says: "Sam can't come to the phone."

You call again tomorrow. Same robot. You never get Sam.

That is what happens to our calls.

5 out of 100 still sounds small. That's because Sam is often busy and his voicemail answers. But at least it's his voicemail, so he can call back.

So what?

4 of every 10 calls we make go to the front desk. That's the phone that almost never works.

Part 2A helper keeps swapping our phone book

Apollo is like a helper who looks after our phone book.

The helper has two rules:

Rule 1. Every time we add someone to our call list, the helper buys a new number for them. Then he writes it over the old one. Even when the old one worked.

Rule 2. If a call to that new number goes badly, the helper buys another new number.

Each new number costs coins (Apollo calls them credits). Nobody checks if the new number actually reaches the person.

So it goes round and round, like a hamster on a wheel:

So what?

We pay coins to throw away numbers that work and buy numbers that don't.

Part 3So we made a notebook

We built a notebook that listens to every sales call.

For each call it writes down who picked up: a person, a robot, a secretary, or voicemail.

For each phone number it remembers: "this one works", "this one is a robot", or "we don't know yet".

It checks for new calls every 20 minutes while the team is working. We tested it by hand, and it was right 97 times out of 100.

Earlier findings (review of 1–21 Sep, published 22 Sep)
